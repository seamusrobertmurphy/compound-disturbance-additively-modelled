# Discussion {#sec-discussion}

## Principal findings {#sec-found}

In the Darkwoods Conservation Area, prior beetle attack did not change the probability that a cell burned at high severity in 2015. The log-odds for attacked against unattacked cells were `r sprintf("%.3f", SEV_ADJ)` (block bootstrap interval `r ci_of(M_ADJ, "y")`) with lodgepole presence and elevation in the model, the interval spanned zero at every block size tried, and the count of attack years showed no hump-shaped effect. Lodgepole presence had the largest coefficient, `r sprintf("%.3f", gv(M_ADJ, "host"))` (`r ci_of(M_ADJ, "host")`), with cells holding the host less likely to burn at high severity.

The compound effect was decisive under the likelihood the parent study used [χ^2^(`r LR_DF`) = `r sprintf("%.1f", LR_STAT)`, `r if (LR_P < 0.001) "p < 0.001" else sprintf("p = %.3f", LR_P)`], with `r N_PROD_SIG` of `r LR_DF` product terms excluding zero, and was not supported under inference that accounted for the clustering of the seedling pattern, under which every product term spanned zero at every block size tried and a cluster-robust sandwich clustered on the same blocks agreed. The estimates did not move and the intervals widened by a factor of `r sprintf("%.1f", WIDEN$lo)` to `r sprintf("%.1f", WIDEN$hi)`, and that widening was the only difference between the two verdicts.

## Linked disturbance {#sec-linked}

The null on the linked effect agreed with every study of the grey stage the review found, whether made in the field, by simulation or from maps. In the field, @talucci2019dead "found no evidence of a relationship between outbreak severity and fire severity for six of seven first-order fire effects, with the exception of deep charring" in three fires of central British Columbia, and @harvey2014 found in six fires of the northern Rocky Mountains that "fire severity was driven primarily by weather and topography, with MPB outbreaks affecting a minority of fire severity measures, primarily under extreme conditions". From maps, @liang2016 found severity "unrelated to pre-fire outbreak severity" across an ecoregion and @hart2015 found area burned unaffected by infestation across the western United States. By simulation, @simard2011 found that the wind needed to sustain active crown fire was higher in grey-stage stands than in undisturbed ones, and @sieg2017 found the grey stage mostly antagonistic and the linkage neutral under high wind, although @jenkins2012 judged the crown fire model of the first inappropriately applied to beetle-killed canopies. The Darkwoods stands burned in the grey stage, and a log-odds of `r sprintf("%.3f", SEV_ADJ)`, with an interval spanning zero, was consistent with those results.

The comparison that qualified the null was with @harvey2014 and @talucci2019dead, who each fitted outbreak severity in a product with burning conditions because the beetle signal, where it appeared, depended on fire weather. @harvey2014 found it only under extreme conditions and @simard2011 only under "certain intermediate weather conditions", and @talucci2019thesis wrote that "In subalpine forests, fuels become relatively less important as a controlling variable when fire weather becomes extreme (Bessie and Johnson 1995), while under moderate fire weather conditions, fuels may be more important for burn severity." A single fire gave no weather contrast, and whether the Mt Midgeley fire burned under moderate or extreme weather was not recorded in these surfaces, so the attack coefficient was an average over unknown burning conditions and could conceal an effect confined to one of them. The hump in the probability of high severity at intermediate outbreak severity [@talucci2019thesis] was likewise estimated after accounting for fire weather. The attack-year count stood in for her continuous outbreak severity and showed no curvature, but the count ran from zero to `r length(YR_LIVE)` over a window that closed four years before the fire, and it measured how long a cell stayed in red attack and not how much of its stand died.

The severity classes carried a measurement caveat that any analysis of them inherited. @lentile2006 reported that high-severity fire produced more homogeneous effects than moderate or low, so at 30 m the moderate class was the most mixed, and @talucci2019thesis noted "discrepancies between field observations and satellite-measured change" where prefire mortality existed. The field studies measured severity on the ground as char height, scorch and tree mortality, and @carlson2017 avoided the classes by analysing high-severity cells alone. Modelling high severity against the rest kept the least mixed class as the response, which was one reason the first objective took that form and not a tabulation of attack across classes.

## Compound disturbance {#sec-compound}

The null on the compound effect agreed with every field study that counted tree seedlings after beetle then fire. @harvey2014 found that "seedling density was unrelated to prefire MPB outbreak severity overall" and within each class of fire severity and outbreak stage, @talucci2019drivers found recruitment driven by fire severity and cone abundance "while substantial underlying tree mortality from the MPB outbreaks did not play a role", and concluded that "Sub-boreal lodgepole pine forests are generally resilient when MPB outbreaks and wildfire recur in short intervals", and @stevensrumann2015 found "no evidence that a beetle and wildfire interaction affected seedling densities" in dry mixed conifer forest. The studies that reported a compound effect measured something other than seedlings, or a different first disturbance. @carlson2017 found lower Landsat vegetation cover two years after fire where spruce beetle outbreak had been more severe, and cautioned that "understory recovery is not necessarily an indicator of overstory regeneration", @liang2016 reported a higher ratio of regrowth area after beetle then fire without a test or an interval and qualified it against @harvey2014, and @buma2011 found fewer conifer seedlings where blowdown had left heavy fuel on the ground before the fire. Set against each other, the two groups suggested that a compound effect on regeneration followed from what the first disturbance did to fuel at the ground surface or appeared in a spectral index, and not in seedling counts after an outbreak that left its dead trees standing.

The field nulls were each qualified by their authors, and the qualifications applied here. @harvey2014 allowed that compound effects "may be possible following high-severity fires in stands where most lodgepole pine trees are dead at the time of fire", which was the case the parent study's abstract described and the feedback @krawchuk2020 set out, in which beetle-killed canopy reduces the seed source a fire refugium supplies. The product of grey-stage attack with high severity was the direct test of that case, and its interval spanned zero at every block size (@fig-interaction, @tbl-ppblocks). @stevensrumann2015 found no seedlings on most of their burned sites and wrote that "any additional reduction in seedling density from previous bark beetle activity may not be visible on these overall low regeneration sites", and @harvey2014 claimed the opposite position, that finding other effects implied "our statistical power to detect possible effects of beetle outbreaks was high". This study made neither claim, and found that the data the parent study built could not support the feedback either way, which meant the additive specification was the right one to have fitted. In the terms of @sieg2017 an additive outcome was the neutral linkage, a compounded effect was in the words of @paine1998 "multiplicative, not additive", and these data could not tell the two apart. The finding therefore revised nothing in the parent study's ecology and concerned only what three plots of clustered seedlings could show about a product term.

## Spatial inference {#sec-inference}

The disagreement between the Poisson fit and the field studies had a cause that was not specific to this landscape. A point process trend fitted by the Berman-Turner device was a weighted Poisson regression [@berman1992; @baddeley2000], and a Poisson likelihood counted every seedling as an independent piece of evidence about the covariates, whereas post-fire regeneration was clustered. @malone2018 wrote that "A clustered spatial pattern can be an indication of species having similar ecological requirements [78], facilitation among individuals [79,80], or dispersal limitations at larger scales [81]", and found regenerating conifers "clustered at distances up to at least 30 m", and @ziegler2017 found that "All species had aggregated spatial patterns, with aggregation greatest at smaller scales" and that their Cox models "underpredicted tree densities at smaller distances". The parent study devoted two figures and 999 Monte Carlo simulations to establishing that the seedling pattern was clustered [@murphy2026], and the residual correlogram of @fig-correlogram reached the same result from inside the model, with dependence of `r PCORR$"Residual correlation"[1]` within 25 m.

The studies of beetle and fire met the same dependence and mostly answered it by design. @buma2011 wrote that "sampling within disturbances necessarily invokes some spatial autocorrelation and pseudoreplication", @simard2011 spaced sites 350 m apart and @harvey2014 plots at least 400 m apart, and each plot then gave one density, so the seedlings within it were never counted separately. @harvey2014 "were unable to fit these data to general or generalized linear models" and tested the compound effect by rank correlation, and @talucci2019drivers fitted one model per factor "due to the relatively small sample size". Two studies carried the dependence into the inference. @carlson2017 fitted a spatial error model because "spatial dependence in either the response or the explanatory variables can violate assumptions of observation independence and inflate parameter values", and still found an effect, and @sieg2017 compared simulations by "a spatially blocked bootstrapping approach". A stem map lost the independence that plot spacing provided, because it turned each plot into thousands of observations, and no study the review found had fitted a product of two disturbances to mapped seedlings. How the intervals of covariate effects in inhomogeneous point process models of regeneration should be computed once clustering was admitted was a question the point pattern studies had not settled, in that @ziegler2017 relied on replication across eighteen plots and a random effect for fire, @malone2018 reported least-squares fits without stating the error model, and the parent study reported Poisson errors. The block bootstrap and cluster-robust sandwich used here were two answers among several, and the size of the correction they returned, a widening of `r sprintf("%.1f", WIDEN$lo)` to `r sprintf("%.1f", WIDEN$hi)`, was what this study contributed to that question.

The same caution had been exercised before fitting by @donato2009, who in modelling conifer regeneration after a large mixed-severity fire excluded from their candidate set any model with more than five predictors or any interaction term, on the stated ground that the sample was too modest to support them, and reported relative importance among additive predictors instead. Their study was of comparable design and larger extent, and its exclusion of interaction terms agreed with what the intervals measured here supported.

## Extent constraints {#sec-extent}

Every result was bounded by the extent of a single burn of about eight square kilometres, inside which the regeneration plots totalled `r sprintf("%.1f", WIN_HA)` ha, which was the ground on which beetle and fire co-occurred, against six fires and 105 plots in @harvey2014, three fires and 83 plots in @talucci2019drivers and four fires and 180 plots in @stevensrumann2015. Across the burn, lodgepole cover and elevation correlated at r = `r sprintf("%.2f", R_PL_EL)` and could not be separated, where the companion study distinguished them because 1,776 m of relief held pine-poor ground at every height [@murphy2026refugia]. The same conflict governed the inference at both scales, because a block bootstrap needed blocks large enough to contain the dependence and numerous enough to resample, and the extent capped the first before the second was met, with `r SWEEP$Blocks[SWEEP$"Block side (m)" == BLOCK_M]` blocks of 500 m across the burn where 1 km would have given eight, and `r PP_OK_TXT` inside the plots where the residual range demanded 75 to 100 m.

Both results were nulls whose meaning was set by the sample, in that they did not show that beetle mortality and burn severity did not interact, and showed only that `r format(N_FR, big.mark = ",")` cells in `r K_500` blocks could not detect a linked effect of the size these surfaces could record, and that `r format(N_DATA, big.mark = ",")` seedlings across `r sprintf("%.1f", WIN_HA)` ha, clustered as this pattern was, could not resolve a difference of differences across four severity-by-attack combinations. Residual dependence that neither the bootstrap nor the sandwich captured would have left every interval still too narrow, which for two null results was a conservative failure, because a fuller accounting could only widen intervals that already spanned zero. The seedlings were mapped five growing seasons after the fire, where @harvey2014 counted theirs after one and noted that "the vast majority of postfire serotinous lodgepole pine recruitment occurs within 1 y of fire", so the Darkwoods pattern included five years of mortality and later establishment that the first-year counts did not.

The first objective was answered inside the perimeter only, because the comparison the linked-disturbance literature makes, between attacked stands that burned and attacked stands that did not, needed beetle surfaces that reached beyond the burn, and recovering it would mean rebuilding the classification over the unclipped landscape. The severity reference category of the regeneration model was inherited and not chosen, because the published specification had indicators for high and low severity only and pooled moderate severity with unburned and unclassified ground into a reference that was `r round(100 * REF_MOD / REF_N)` per cent moderate, and it was reproduced and not repaired, because the object was to test the published model and not to replace it. The provincial survey was a coarse instrument, its polygons sketched from an aircraft at a mapping scale far coarser than 30 m, and it was used only to test whether ground outside the perimeter was attacked at all, which it could answer, and not to measure severity, which it could not.

## Research priorities {#sec-next}

Three tasks followed, in descending order of value against cost, of which the first was to repeat the compound-effect test where extent did not limit the inference, which given the dependence range measured here meant a network of plots spaced beyond that range, as @simard2011 and @harvey2014 spaced theirs, and not three contiguous plots. The second was to report the published point process results with intervals that respected the clustering, which was inexpensive because the estimates were unchanged and which on the evidence of @fig-interaction left two main effects with intervals that excluded zero. The third was to test the linked effect with fire weather as a covariate, in the form of @harvey2014 and @talucci2019thesis, which required more than one fire or a fire whose weather record spanned the burn, and rebuilding the beetle classification over the unclipped landscape would restore the comparison across the perimeter that these surfaces could not make.

# Conclusions {#sec-conclusions}

This study tested, on the data of the parent study, the two effects the field distinguishes between a beetle outbreak and the fire that follows it, and found neither, in agreement with the field studies of lodgepole pine at short intervals. Prior attack did not change the probability of high-severity burning within the Mt Midgeley burn, at `r sprintf("%.3f", SEV_ADJ)` in log-odds after conditioning on host and elevation with an interval spanning zero at every block size the extent supported, and the count of attack years showed no hump. The beetle by fire product terms were decisive under the published Poisson likelihood and absent under inference that accounted for the clustering the parent study itself documented, with identical coefficients either way and intervals that differed by a factor of `r sprintf("%.1f", WIDEN$lo)` to `r sprintf("%.1f", WIDEN$hi)`.

The additive model of the parent study was therefore the defensible one, because nothing found here supported modelling beetle and fire any other way with data of this extent, and the apparent support for doing so came from a likelihood that counted clustered seedlings as independent observations. A compound effect of beetle and fire on regeneration in this landscape was not demonstrated and could not have been with these data, and the result that applied beyond this landscape was how far the intervals widened once the clustering of the seedlings was accounted for.
