# Introduction

Mountain pine beetle (*Dendroctonus ponderosae* Hopkins) outbreak and wildfire have overlapped across the conifer forests of western North America since the late 1990s, and the field has kept two questions about such a pair apart. @simard2011 set out both, in that "Compound disturbances (sensu Paine et al. 1998) take place when two disturbances occurring in a short period of time have a synergistic effect that cannot be predicted from the sum of the individual disturbances", whereas "a disturbance may interact with another by changing its extent, severity, or probability of occurrence", which they called linked disturbances. @paine1998 had put the first idea in arithmetic terms, in that "the community effect of compounded perturbations is multiplicative, not additive", and @sieg2017 gave the alternative its name, classing a pair whose combined effect matched the sum of the two as "neutral (also called additive)". In this literature an interaction was therefore a dependence between events, an additive outcome was a named result, and a product term was one of several ways of testing between them. The present study kept those meanings and used product term for the statistical quantity alone.

The linked question, whether an outbreak changed the fire that followed, was asked in the field, by simulation and from maps, and none of the three found a general amplification in the grey stage, after the dead needles had dropped. @jenkins2012 reviewed the fuel changes through an outbreak and judged them predictable but "not yet rigorously quantified", and @meigs2011 mapped beetle effects on tree mortality and surface fuels from Landsat time series. In the field, @harvey2014 sampled 105 plots in six fires of 2011 in lodgepole pine (*Pinus contorta* Douglas ex Loudon var. *latifolia* Engelm. ex S. Watson) of the northern Rocky Mountains and found that "fire severity was driven primarily by weather and topography, with MPB outbreaks affecting a minority of fire severity measures, primarily under extreme conditions", and @talucci2019dead sampled 63 plots in three fires of 2012 to 2014 in central British Columbia, about a decade after the outbreak, and "found no evidence of a relationship between outbreak severity and fire severity for six of seven first-order fire effects, with the exception of deep charring". By simulation, @simard2011 found that the wind speed needed to sustain active crown fire rose from 39 km/h in undisturbed stands to 61 km/h in the red stage and 71 km/h in the grey stage, a dampening, although @jenkins2012 judged the crown fire model inappropriately applied to beetle-killed canopies, and @sieg2017 found the red stage synergistic at low wind and the grey stage mostly antagonistic. From maps, @hart2015 found across 546 large fires that "the annual area burned across the western United States is unaffected by MPB infestation", and @liang2016 found across the Southern Rocky Mountains that "The severity of fire was unrelated to pre-fire outbreak severity, and no evidence of MPB mortality increasing fire occurrence was found." The one positive result for the grey stage was the Landsat analysis of @talucci2019thesis, in which "intermediate levels of outbreak generate a fuel arrangement that supports the greatest probability of high severity fire on these landscapes", after accounting for fire weather and terrain.

The compound question, whether a prior outbreak changed what regenerated after the fire, was answered with no effect by the three field studies that counted tree seedlings, in that @harvey2014 found that "seedling density was unrelated to prefire MPB outbreak severity overall" and within each class of fire severity and outbreak stage, @talucci2019drivers found in 83 plots that "Fire severity and cone abundance were the primary drivers of recruitment density in burned stands, while substantial underlying tree mortality from the MPB outbreaks did not play a role", and @stevensrumann2015 found in 180 plots of dry mixed conifer forest "no evidence that a beetle and wildfire interaction affected seedling densities". The studies that found an effect measured a vegetation index, an area of regrowth or a different first disturbance, in that @carlson2017 modelled a Landsat vegetation index two years after fire in Colorado and found "more severe spruce beetle outbreaks corresponding to reduced post-fire vegetation cover", while cautioning that "understory recovery is not necessarily an indicator of overstory regeneration", @liang2016 compared ratios of regrowth area without a test or an interval, and @buma2011 found fewer conifer seedlings where blowdown, not beetle, had left heavy fuel on the ground before the fire. The field nulls were qualified by their authors, since @harvey2014 allowed that compound effects "may be possible following high-severity fires in stands where most lodgepole pine trees are dead at the time of fire", and the mechanism that case rested on was a feedback within the refugia framework of @krawchuk2020, in which "insect-caused mortality of mature trees could reduce the capacity of fire refugia to serve as seed sources for post-fire tree regeneration".

Both questions were contested partly on inference, because prior disturbance was not assigned at random, both causes tracked host abundance and terrain, and severity and seedlings alike were measured at locations far from independent. @buma2011 wrote that "sampling within disturbances necessarily invokes some spatial autocorrelation and pseudoreplication", and most field studies answered by spacing their plots, 350 m apart in @simard2011 and at least 400 m in @harvey2014. @carlson2017 and @sieg2017 carried the dependence into the inference itself, the first by fitting a spatial error model because "spatial dependence in either the response or the explanatory variables can violate assumptions of observation independence and inflate parameter values", and the second by comparing simulations with "a spatially blocked bootstrapping approach". @stevensrumann2016, asking the linked question with fire in place of beetle, fitted a spatial autoregression because burn severity was autocorrelated, @mcintire2006 noted of beetle and fire boundaries in British Columbia that "because of lack of independence of data points, the effective degrees of freedom is less than the number of sample plots", and @magnussen2017 showed in forest inventory that omitting residual spatial autocorrelation left confidence intervals too narrow, so a product term judged by such intervals would be retained too readily. Within the point process studies of post-fire regeneration the covariate effects were reported under Poisson error, by @ziegler2017, who fitted "an inhomogeneous Cox process model, a weighted generalized linear model with a log-link and Poisson error distribution", by @malone2018, and by the parent study of this analysis, and none of the three reported intervals that accounted for the clustering all three documented.

That parent study [@murphy2026] modelled conifer regeneration across three four-hectare plots inside the 2015 Mt Midgeley burn in the Darkwoods Conservation Area, British Columbia, from classified Landsat surfaces of burn severity and of red-stage and grey-stage beetle mortality. Its abstract reported that "areas of overlapping beetle mortality and high burn severity had significantly reduced lodgepole pine regeneration", and its conclusions that the results suggested "the ecological consequences of beetle-fire interactions weaken as surface fuels decay over time". Both were statements of a compound effect, and the first described the case @harvey2014 had left open. The fitted trend entered eight covariates additively, seed source distance, aspect, terrain ruggedness, wind, grey-stage and red-stage beetle mortality, and high and low burn severity, and no product of a beetle indicator with a fire indicator entered the pooled fit or any of the seven per-species fits. The parent study read its compound statements from main effects, which an additive fit permitted, and opened a discussion of how far inherited raster layers and a fitted trend could support such a claim. This paper ran the tests that discussion called for, on the same data, against the position the field studies above had reached.

The objectives of this study were to (1) test the linked effect, whether prior beetle attack changed the probability of high-severity burning within the Mt Midgeley burn after accounting for host cover and elevation, and whether the hump-shaped form @talucci2019thesis reported recurred, and (2) test the compound effect, whether prior beetle attack changed the regeneration response to burn severity, by refitting the published point process model with beetle by fire product terms and comparing it against the published additive fit under inference that accounted for the clustering of the seedlings.

The wording was kept narrow throughout, because a designed factorial could justify the language of interaction outright, as @bansal2014 did in crossing burn severity with canopy cover, and @talucci2019dead retained their weather by outbreak product terms "regardless of statistical significance, because of the known interaction between fire weather and fuels", whereas an observational fit over ground that neither treatment nor randomisation touched could not. This study therefore reported whether each term was retained and what interval its coefficient had, and did not report that the two causes interacted. The Methods described the surfaces, the limits they placed on what could be compared, and the two models, the Results reported the severity model (@sec-severity) and the regeneration model (@sec-interaction), and the Discussion set each against the studies above and closed with the inferential point, that the product terms were decisive under the likelihood the published model was fitted with and absent under inference that accounted for the clustering the parent study itself documented, with the same coefficients either way.
